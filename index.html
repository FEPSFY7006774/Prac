Introduction

The ocean covers around 70% of the Earth's surface and yet only 5% of the ocean has been explored with 27.3% of the global seafloor being mapped [Bristol Aquarium, 2026]. This makes the ocean a massive point of interest for humanity, but we cannot explore further without the help of technology. As depth increases underwater vehicles become necessary for sea exploration as they are able to overcome; human physical limitations, extreme pressures, hazardous/ remote environments and carry out research without disturbing surrounding wildlife. In this paper underwater vehicles are separated into the different aspects that make them a versatile research tool.

Drag and hull shape

Hull design is the engineering of a watercraft’s shape to control how it interacts and navigates efficiently through water. Depending on the vessel's function hull design needs to balance between hydrostatics (buoyancy and stability) and hydrodynamics (speed, drag and how effective it is at cutting through water). A hull shape of an underwater vehicle can be split into the nose, middle, tail and propulsion with most AUVs (autonomous underwater vehicles) being designed based on the Myring hull profile equations [Myring, 1976] which are known to produce minimum drag force [ScienceDirect, 2012]. Drag on underwater vehicles occurs when water is flowing against the vessel. This difference creates a backward force called pressure drag. As the vessel speeds up, it experiences a greater drag force due to it colliding with more water particles per second. This can create problems on longer duration expeditions as more energy would be required to push the water particles aside, making hull design even more important. The majority of modern day underwater vehicles are designed to be shaped like a torpedo/ teardrop (rounded blunt nose with a smoothly tapered tail). This is because it offers the lowest hydrodynamic drag and highest energy efficiency for long distance or high speed cruising.

Pressure

Underwater pressure is the force that is exerted from all directions against an object. The greater the depth the heavier the column of water above becomes, causing the pressure to increase rapidly. For every10m you go down, the pressure increases by one atmosphere [NOAA, 2026], meaning during deep sea exploration the pressure reaches hundreds of atmospheres which can crush standard vessels if not built to withstand it. To survive the hydrostatic pressures forces underwater vehicles typically use spherical shapes to distribute compressive forces evenly, alongside high strength materials such as titanium due to their high strength to weight ratio and resistance to deformation. This was proven in 2023 when Oceangate’s Titan sub dive to 3,800m went tragically wrong and five lives were lost. One of the key reasons the Titan sub failed was its unusual hull shape which was cylindrical rather than spherical, so the crushing pressure of the deep was distributed unequally [BBC, 2024].

Propulsion

To travel through the ocean, underwater vehicles rely on some form of propulsion, with propellers being the most widely used choice. This is because they are simple, reliable, cheap and easy to seal against pressure in low depths. A propeller's role is to push water backwards, and by Newton’s Third Law (every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force) the vehicle moves forwards. The propeller creates its thrust by using curved blades that act as rotating wings to create a pressure difference, pulling water in from the front and driving a high speed stream out the back. Engineers have and are trying to emulate a fish’s scale and body shape due to their better suited ability for sliding through the water then submarines. However, it is a complicated process making all the linkages and sophisticated robotic design to get an underwater vessel that traverses like a fish [MIT, 2008].

Biomimetic design

Biomimetic design is vital in underwater vehicles as in deep sea it is important to not damage or disturb the surrounding wildlife, both for the quality of research and to protect the vessel from predators. Biomimetic design is the process of using nature inspired engineering to solve human challenges or protect wildlife. An example of the use of biomimetic design in an underwater vehicle is shark skin inspired boundary layer on a vessel. Sharks are covered in tiny tooth-like scales called denticles that overlap in precise patterns that channels water smoothly across the body. This breaks up turbulence and reduces drag, allowing sharks to swim faster and quieter which are all beneficial to underwater vehicles. Another biomimetic design used in underwater vehicles is Jellyfish jet inspired propulsion as they are one of the most efficient marine animals on the planet when considering the power to speed and weight ratio [University of Southampton, 2021]. Robots use flexible limbs which form together a propulsive bell to mimic the swimming pattern of a jelly fish and produce jets of fluid to propel the vessel through the water.

Conclusion

Going forward underwater vehicles are a necessity for discovering more of the ocean as they provide opportunities that would not be available for humans. The deeper the operation the more specialised and equipped the vessels need to be to overcome problems such as pressure, efficiency and environment. Overtime engineers have found many ways to create solutions from; altering the shape, using specific materials or copying sea creature attributes. Each with its own purpose and each better from some tasks more then others.
